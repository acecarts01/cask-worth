VSOP vs XO Cognac: What the Grades Mean, What They Cost and Which to Buy

By Caskworth Editorial Published August 11, 2026Updated November 7, 20269 min read

The VSOP vs XO question comes down to age, depth and price. Under the rules of the Cognac trade body, VSOP means the youngest eau-de-vie in the blend is at least 4 years old, and XO means at least 10, which is why an XO costs about three to three and a half times as much as a VSOP from the same house. This guide explains what each grade means, what changed in 2018, how the prices compare across Hennessy, Rémy Martin, Martell, Courvoisier and Camus, and which grade suits a cocktail, a first neat pour or a gift.

What VS, VSOP and XO Mean

The letters on a cognac label are not marketing shorthand. The Cognac trade body, the BNIC, lists the age terms on its protected origin page. A cognac described as Very Special has aged at least 2 years, Very Superior Old Pale (VSOP) at least 4 years, and Extra Old (XO) at least 10 years. The same page lists Napoléon and similar terms at 6 years and says XXO or Extra Extra Old is specific to cognacs aged 14 years or more.

Two details matter. First, the BNIC says the age of a spirit corresponds to the youngest component of the blending, so one young eau-de-vie caps the grade no matter how old the rest is. Second, cognac cannot be sold before a minimum of two years of ageing, without interruption and exclusively in oak vessels. The letters are a floor, not an average, so a good house usually blends older spirits than the label requires.

Other Terms on Cognac Labels

VS, VSOP and XO are the grades most buyers see, but a label can use other words that sit in the same tiers. The BNIC lists them by minimum age, so you can decode a label without guessing:

  • At least 2 years: 3 Étoiles, Sélection, De Luxe and Very Special.
  • At least 4 years: Réserve, Vieux, Rare, Royal and Very Superior Old Pale.
  • At least 6 years: Napoléon, Très Vieille Réserve, Très Vieux, Héritage, Très Rare, Excellence and Suprême.
  • At least 10 years: Hors d’âge, Extra, Ancestral, Ancêtre, Or, Gold, Impérial and Extra Old.

That means a bottle called Hors d’âge or Extra is in the same minimum-age tier as an XO, and a bottle called Réserve sits with the VSOP. Brand names such as Excellence or Imperial are not grades on their own, so always check which tier the term belongs to. If a bottle has no grade at all, ask the seller for the producer’s own description before you buy.

The 2018 Change to the XO Minimum

If you read older guides, you may see XO described as six years. That was the rule until the BNIC changed it. Trade publication Harper’s reported that the XO minimum rose from six to ten years on 1 April 2018, so the youngest eau-de-vie in a blended XO must now have been aged at least ten years. The report says the BNIC wanted to align the regulation with market reality, since many XOs already contained eau-de-vie older than ten years, and that XOs aged six to nine years and pre-packaged by 31 March 2018 could be sold until 31 March 2019. You can read the original in Harper’s report on the XO age change.

The practical lesson: when you compare vsop vs xo today, the gap is at least six years at the minimum, which is a real difference in cost and depth, and an old bottle bought secondhand may follow the old rule.

The Difference Between VS and VSOP

Many first-time buyers ask about the difference between VS and VSOP, because the price gap is small. Within the same houses, the VSOP costs $15 to $25 more than the VS: Courvoisier is $34.99 for the VS and $49.99 for the VSOP, Martell is $34.99 and $59.99, Camus is $39.99 and $59.99, and Hennessy is $39.99 and $59.99. For that small step you get a minimum age of 4 years instead of 2.

That makes the VSOP the usual best-value grade for drinking neat, and the VS the natural choice when you are mixing. The Cocktail Craft video VS vs VSOP vs XO: The Ultimate Cognac Guide walks through the three grades in about five minutes if you prefer to watch.

How VSOP and XO Differ in the Glass

Age shows up in the glass, but the label letters do not describe the flavour for you, so use them as a guide, not a promise. In general, a younger blend tastes brighter and more fruit-forward, while an older blend tends to be rounder, with more oak, dried fruit and spice and a longer finish. How much of that you taste depends on the house, the blend and the bottle.

A fair way to compare is to taste a VSOP and an XO from the same house side by side. Pour a small measure of each into a tulip glass, nose them, taste the VSOP first and then the XO, and add a few drops of water if the strength feels warm. If you cannot tell the difference, buy the VSOP and keep the money.

Two common mistakes are worth avoiding. The first is paying for an XO and then mixing it with cola or ginger ale, which hides exactly what the extra years add. The second is assuming a higher price within the same grade means an older cognac: the grade is a minimum, so compare bottles by the producer’s own description and your own taste, not by price alone.

VSOP or XO: Which Should You Buy?

If you are still deciding on VSOP or XO, use this guide:

  • Cocktails and mixed drinks: choose a VS. It is the cheapest grade and is designed to hold up in mixers.
  • A first neat pour or a good everyday sipper: choose a VSOP at $49.99 to $59.99. This is the best balance of age and price.
  • A gift, a celebration or a collection: choose an XO, from $179.99. The extra age, at least ten years for the youngest spirit, and the fuller presentation justify the premium.
  • You want to go further: look at older and rarer bottles in our cognac collection.

VSOP vs XO Brandy: Is Cognac the Same Thing?

People also search for vsop vs xo brandy and xo vs vsop brandy, and the answer is that cognac is a protected type of brandy. Brandy is the broad category of spirits distilled from fruit, usually grapes. Cognac is brandy made in a delimited area of western France under strict rules, and the VS, VSOP and XO grades described by the BNIC apply to cognac specifically. Other brandies use their own terms, and some borrow the same letters, so check the rules for the region before you assume the letters mean the same thing.

The BNIC also explains that Fine Champagne is a cognac made exclusively from Grande Champagne, at least 50%, and Petite Champagne. If you see Fine Champagne on a label, it describes the origin of the spirit, not its age.
