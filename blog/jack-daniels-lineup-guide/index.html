What Makes Jack Daniel's Tennessee Whiskey

Tennessee whiskey is straight whiskey made in Tennessee. According to Wikipedia’s Tennessee whiskey article, a 2013 law (House Bill 1084, signed by Governor Bill Haslam on 13 May 2013) requires the Lincoln County Process for anything labelled Tennessee whiskey, together with Tennessee origin and the basic bourbon requirements. Those include a mash of at least 51% corn and ageing in new charred oak, rules you can read for bourbon in 27 CFR 5.143.

The extra step is the charcoal. Wikipedia’s article on Jack Daniel’s says the whiskey is filtered through ten-foot stacks of sugar maple charcoal before it goes into the barrel, a step the company calls mellowing. So Jack Daniel’s sits in the bourbon family legally, but the charcoal step is why Tennessee whiskey is its own label. Our bourbon vs whiskey guide explains the wider rules.

The same article says the brand is owned by Brown-Forman, which bought it in 1956, and notes that the label reads “Est. & Reg. in 1866” while a biographer’s research on registration documents points to 1875. We mention the dispute because accurate history matters when you are choosing a bottle as a gift.

The Core Straight Whiskeys

Jack Daniel’s Old No. 7 at $22.99 and 40% is the standard bottle and the reference point for the range. Wikipedia notes that the Black Label was 90 proof until 1987 and is now 80 proof, so older bottles you see elsewhere may differ. Anyone who types Jack Daniel’s Old No 7 into a search box will find this one, and it is the usual choice for a highball or a simple cocktail.

Jack Daniel’s Gentleman Jack at $29.99 and 40% is described by Wikipedia as charcoal filtered twice instead of once. That is the documented difference, and it is why people describe it as the smoother step up. The strength is the same as Old No. 7, so you are paying for the process, not for extra proof.

Jack Daniel’s Single Barrel at $49.99 and 47% is 94 proof according to Wikipedia. Each bottle comes from a single barrel, so expect small differences from bottle to bottle, and anyone shopping for Jack Daniels Single Barrel should check the strength on the label. The 10 Year at $49.99 and 48.5% is listed in our catalogue as a Tennessee whiskey with a 10 year age statement; read its product page for the details. Wikipedia also lists a barrel proof single barrel bottling, a different product that we do not currently stock.

Jack Daniel’s Sinatra Select at $149.99 and 45% is the top of our range. Wikipedia describes it as 90 proof and named for Frank Sinatra, so a search for Jack Daniels Sinatra Select leads to this one bottle. Our Jack Daniel’s Rye Whiskey at $39.99 is listed at 45%, and Wikipedia gives the Tennessee Rye as 90 proof, so the two agree. If you like spicy whiskeys, it is the one to try first.

The Flavoured Bottles

Tennessee Honey, Tennessee Fire and Tennessee Apple are each $27.99 and 35% in our catalogue. Wikipedia describes the three as 70 proof liqueurs blended with less than 20% whiskey, not as straight whiskeys, so treat them as a separate category from the bottles above. If you are searching for a Jack Daniel’s Tennessee Honey whiskey, the product is honey flavoured and sweeter by design.

These bottles suit people who find straight whiskey too sharp, and they work over ice or in simple mixed drinks. They are not a stepping stone in strength, since at 35% they are the lowest in the range. If your goal is to learn what Tennessee whiskey tastes like, start with Old No. 7 or Gentleman Jack instead.

A good rule when buying gifts is to ask what the recipient already drinks. Someone who likes sweet liqueurs will enjoy the flavoured bottles, while someone who drinks bourbon or rye will usually prefer a straight whiskey. If you are unsure, the Old No. 7 at $22.99 is the safe choice.

How to Serve and Taste Them

A few simple habits get more from each bottle. For the straight whiskeys, pour about an ounce into a small tulip or rocks glass and let it sit for a minute before nosing it. Take a small first sip neat, then add a few drops of water or a single large ice cube and taste again. The extra water opens the aroma, especially at 47% and above.

Old No. 7 and Gentleman Jack work well in a highball with soda or ginger ale, and in an Old Fashioned with sugar and bitters. The rye suits a Manhattan, where its spice stands up to sweet vermouth. The Single Barrel and the 10 Year are better kept for neat sipping, since mixing them hides what the extra strength and age are for. Sinatra Select is a gift bottle for most buyers, so serve it neat or over a single cube.

The flavoured bottles are easiest to enjoy cold. Serve Tennessee Honey over ice, Tennessee Apple with soda and Tennessee Fire as a chilled shot or with a mixer, and keep them separate from the straight whiskeys when you plan a tasting, because their sweetness will dominate the palate.

Storage is the same for all nine: keep bottles upright, away from heat and direct light, and reseal after pouring. An opened bottle of whiskey lasts a long time, but the flavour slowly changes with air, so finish a bottle within a year or two if you want it at its best. If you are setting up a small home bar, one straight whiskey and one flavoured bottle cover most guests.

Visiting the Distillery and the Story Behind It

Wikipedia says the Jack Daniel’s visitor centre was dedicated in June 2000, with a gift shop, paid tours and a premium sampling tour, and that the distillery draws more than 250,000 visitors a year. A $140 million expansion was announced in February 2016. If you are planning a Jack Daniel’s distillery tour, check the official site for current tour options and booking, since details change.
