What Wheated Bourbon Is

Bourbon must be at least 51% corn, as 27 CFR 5.143 sets out, and the rest of the mash bill is the distiller’s choice. In most bourbon the secondary grain is rye. In wheated bourbon it is wheat. Wikipedia’s Maker’s Mark article gives the best-known example: a mash of 70% corn, 16% red winter wheat and 14% malted barley, with no rye at all, which it calls unusual.

The result is usually described as softer and rounder, though that is a tendency, not a rule, because barrels, age and strength matter too. Buffalo Trace says the same of its Weller line on its Daniel Weller page: wheat replaces rye as the secondary grain, giving a softer, smoother profile, and the brand keeps the wheated mash concept that William Larue Weller introduced in the 19th century.

For a short video explainer, Beginning Bourbon’s What is a “Wheater / Wheated” Bourbon? is a good companion. This wheated bourbon guide uses the facts above to compare bottles you can buy.

Wheated vs Rye-Forward Bourbon

Rye adds spice and bite, and wheat tends to leave a softer, sweeter profile, so wheated vs rye bourbon flavor is largely a question of how much pepper you want. High rye bourbon puts more rye into the mash, so the high rye vs wheated bourbon comparison is the clearest contrast in American whiskey: one leans spicy, the other leans soft.

That is a generalisation. Strength, age and cask all change flavour, so a high-proof wheated bourbon such as Larceny Barrel Proof at 61% will taste bolder than a lower-proof rye-forward bottle. The only reliable test is to taste one of each side by side. Our bourbon vs rye vs Scotch guide sets out the rules for each style.

A fair test uses two bottles at similar strength and price, for example Maker’s Mark Original at $29.99 and a rye-forward bourbon at about the same price. Pour half an ounce of each, nose gently, and taste the softer one first. Note whether you prefer the sweetness or the spice, and you will know which side to buy from next.

The Daniel Weller Ancient-Wheat Experiments

Buffalo Trace’s Daniel Weller page says the experimental line launched in 2023 and explores how different wheat strains affect bourbon’s character. The first release used emmer wheat, which the page calls an ancient Egyptian grain rarely seen in modern distilling. It was aged for nearly 12 years and bottled at 94 proof (47% ABV), with a nose of orange zest, hazelnut, cinnamon and caramel, a palate of oak, honey and orchard fruits, and an oak-forward finish. The page names Daniel Weller as the grandfather of William Larue Weller.

The second release, announced on 24 March 2026, uses spelt wheat. The press release, reproduced by Breaking Bourbon, says spelt replaces rye as the secondary grain in the Weller mash bill, that it is 10 years old at 94 proof, and that it is lightly sweet and gently nutty. The nose is described as nutty and biscuit-forward with cookies, clove, cinnamon and nutmeg. The suggested retail price is $549.99 for 750 ml, and distribution is limited. The release says each edition tests one variable, here a wheat varietal.

We do not currently list either Daniel Weller bottle. We cover them because they show how much a single grain can change a wheated bourbon, and why searches for Daniel Weller spelt wheat and Daniel Weller emmer wheat have grown. These bottles are a kind of experimental mash bill whiskey, and a useful reminder that wheated is a family of styles, not one flavour. If you want a wheated barrel proof bottle that we do list, our William Larue Weller review covers it.

A Wheated Tasting Flight

The cheapest way to understand wheated bourbon is a flight. Choose three bottles with different strengths, for example Maker’s Mark Original at 45%, Larceny Small Batch at 46% and Larceny Barrel Proof at 61%, which together cost $109.97. Pour half an ounce of each into matching glasses, and taste from the lowest strength to the highest so that the strongest does not overwhelm the others.

Nose each glass gently before sipping, because a hard sniff of 61% spirit mostly registers alcohol. Take a small first sip, hold it on the tongue, and notice whether you taste sweetness first or spice first. Add a few drops of water to the barrel proof bottle and taste again, since water often opens up a high-strength whiskey. Write one line per glass.

Most drinkers find that wheated bourbon shows its sweetness early, with caramel and brown sugar, and that the strongest bottle adds depth without much more spice. If you taste the same flight beside a rye-forward bourbon, the difference in bite becomes obvious, and you will know which style to buy more of.

Mistakes to Avoid

A few errors come up often when buying wheated bourbon:

  • Assuming every smooth bourbon is wheated. Check the mash bill, not just the reputation.
  • Ignoring strength. A 61% bottle will taste bolder than a 45% one, whatever the grain.
  • Chasing rare bottles first. Learn the style with a $29.99 bottle before paying for an allocated one.
  • Trusting an unverified mash bill. If a bottle does not state its recipe, ask the distillery.
  • Over-diluting. A few drops of water are enough for most bottles.

One more point is storage. Keep bottles upright, away from heat and light, and reseal them after each pour. If you are buying as a gift, a wheated bourbon is a safe choice for someone who finds rye too sharp, and the lower-priced bottles in the table make it easy to buy several without a large spend.
