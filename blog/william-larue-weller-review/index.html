What William Larue Weller Is

William Larue Weller is one of the whiskeys in Buffalo Trace’s Antique Collection, a group of limited releases that arrives each fall. On its own page, the distillery calls it a rich, full-bodied wheated bourbon, labelled Kentucky straight bourbon whiskey, and says each release is limited and highly sought after. You can read it at Buffalo Trace’s William Larue Weller page.

Wheated means wheat replaces rye as the secondary grain after corn. Buffalo Trace says the Weller brand keeps the wheated mash bill idea that its namesake introduced in the 19th century. The Antique Collection page adds that Weller was born in 1825 and developed the brand’s wheated recipe, using wheat in place of rye for a smoother taste. It is still bourbon under US law, so the 51% corn and charred new oak rules in 27 CFR 5.143 apply.

The collection itself is larger than many buyers expect. The same page lists six whiskeys, including George T. Stagg, Thomas H. Handy Sazerac Straight Rye, Eagle Rare 17 Year, Sazerac Rye 18 Year and, from 2025, an E.H. Taylor Jr. Bottled-in-Bond bourbon. Our allocated bourbon guide explains why bottles like these are hard to find.

William Larue Weller Review: Tasting Notes From the Distillery

Buffalo Trace’s page gives these notes for the bottling it currently lists: on the nose, caramel corn, new leather, plums, light toffee and pipe tobacco; on the palate, marshmallow, salted almonds, nougat, figs and dates; and a smooth, composed, sweet finish. We have not poured the 2026 bottle ourselves, so we are repeating the producer’s notes, not our own.

The same page lists awards, including a 2025 silver from the International Wine & Spirits Competition and a 2024 double gold from the Global Whiskey Challenge, and notes that Whisky Advocate gave it 95 points and Paul Pacult’s Spirit Journal gave it five stars. Treat scores as one opinion among many.

For a spoken review, Bourbon Forage Fishing’s William LaRue Weller Bourbon Review 2022 & 2023 shows how earlier releases compare. Because each year’s release uses different barrels, a review of one year is a guide, not a guarantee.

Proof Changes Every Release

Buffalo Trace’s page lists 128 proof, or 64% ABV. Our catalogue lists William Larue Weller Barrel Proof at 62.4%, or 124.8 proof, which does not match. The most likely reason is that the strength differs between annual releases, because barrel proof bottles are not cut to a standard strength. Always check the strength printed on the specific bottle you receive, and ask which release year it is before you pay.

That variation is part of the appeal and part of the risk. A higher proof tastes hotter and more concentrated, and a few drops of water help. The bottles in this family run from about 53.5% for Weller Antique 107 to 64.1% for our George T. Stagg Barrel Proof, so strength alone tells you little about quality.

If you see a very different figure from a seller, ask for a photo of the label and the release year. A reputable seller will be happy to provide both.

Where It Sits in the Weller Family

Start with the cheapest bottle. Weller Antique 107 at $84.99 and 53.5% is the entry point to the wheated range in our catalogue. Next, Weller Full Proof at $219.99 and 57% is the step up in strength, and our Weller Full Proof review compares it in detail.

At the top, William Larue Weller Barrel Proof at $499.99 competes with George T. Stagg Barrel Proof at $449.99, another Antique Collection bottle. The two sit close in price, and William Larue Weller is the wheated one, since Buffalo Trace says Weller bourbons use wheat in place of rye as the secondary grain. In our catalogue, Stagg is the slightly stronger at 64.1%, so a side-by-side tasting of both is the clearest way to compare them. Anyone searching for the best wheated bourbon should note that William Larue Weller is the barrel proof option and the Weller 12 Year is another allocated choice.

Collectors who want a single bourbon from this family usually decide between price and strength. If you want the most concentrated wheated bourbon and accept the cost, buy William Larue Weller; that is the usual logic behind a Weller barrel proof buy. If you want to taste the style first, begin with the Antique 107.

A Simple Tasting Plan

A bottle at this price deserves a plan. Pour half an ounce into a tulip glass and let it sit for a few minutes, because high-proof bourbon needs time to settle. Nose it gently from a distance first, then closer, and note what you find before you taste anything. Buffalo Trace’s notes give you a checklist: caramel corn, leather, plums, toffee and tobacco.

Take a very small first sip and hold it on the tongue before swallowing. At 62% or more, the first taste is mostly heat, so do not judge on that. Add two or three drops of water, wait a minute, and taste again. Many drinkers find the flavours of figs, dates and nougat appear once the proof is softened. Repeat with a little more water if needed, but stop before the whiskey turns thin.

Then compare it with a lower-proof wheated bourbon, such as Weller Antique 107, poured the same way. The difference between 53.5% and the barrel proof bottle is easier to understand when you taste them one after the other, and it is a better test of whether the price is worth it to you than any review. Write a line or two about each, so you can look back on your own notes the next time you consider a purchase. Keep the notes with the bottle, because memory fades faster than you expect.

Common Mistakes With Allocated Bourbon

Collectors and first-time buyers make the same few errors with limited releases. Avoid these:

  • Trusting a number without checking the label. Strength changes by release, as the 62.4% and 64% figures above show.
  • Buying on the score alone. A 95-point review tells you what one critic thought, not what you will like.
  • Skipping the cheaper bottle. The Antique 107 teaches you the wheated style for under $100.
  • Opening and leaving. Reseal after each pour and store the bottle upright, away from heat and light.
  • Paying without asking about provenance. Allocated bottles are a favourite of counterfeiters, so ask where the bottle came from.

One more point is storage. A sealed bottle stored properly will keep for years, but an opened bottle slowly changes with air, so finish it within a sensible period if flavour matters more to you than display. If you are buying as a gift, make sure the recipient is 21 or older and enjoys bourbon, because this is not a bottle to give casually.
