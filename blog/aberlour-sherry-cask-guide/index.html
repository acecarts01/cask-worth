Sherry Cask Maturation Explained

Sherry cask maturation means ageing whisky in oak casks that previously held sherry, so the wood passes on dried fruit, raisin and spice, which is why a premium sherry whisky is often described as rich and fruity. Aberlour is a Speyside single malt, and the Scotch rules apply: the Scotch Whisky Association says all Scotch must be matured in oak for at least three years and bottled at 40% or more, and that a bottle does not keep ageing once bottled, as its FAQ explains.

Multiple retailer listings describe the 12 Year Double Cask as matured in both American oak and Oloroso sherry casks, and describe A’bunadh as matured in Oloroso sherry casks, non-chill-filtered and bottled at cask strength in numbered batches. We could not read the distillery’s own pages, so treat these as descriptions repeated by sellers, and check the label of the bottle you buy. For a second opinion on A’bunadh, Gwhisky’s film Aberlour A’bunadh Batch 80 REVIEW is a useful independent view.

A'bunadh: The Cask Strength Bottle

Aberlour abunadh, as most drinkers type it, is released in numbered batches, and the strength varies from batch to batch. Retailer listings for individual batches give strengths between about 59.8% and 61.5%, and our catalogue lists 61.2% for the bottle we stock. If you want to know a batch number or exact strength, read the label, because that detail changes. Because it is bottled undiluted, taste it carefully. Pour a small measure into a tulip glass, nose it gently, take a tiny first sip neat, and then add water a few drops at a time. Reading the Price Ladder

Price per year of age is a rough way to compare the three age-stated bottles. The 12 Year is about $3.75 a year of age, the 16 Year about $5.62 and the 18 Year about $8.33. The cost per year rises as the age does, which is the normal pattern in whisky because older stock costs more to hold and there is less of it. The A’bunadh has no age statement, so this measure does not apply, but its price per pour of about $2.80 sits between the 12 and the 16.

The ladder helps you decide where to stop. If the 12 satisfies you, there is no reason to climb. If you find yourself wanting more depth, the 16 is a gentle step, and the 18 is the top of our range. The reason is that every extra year in the cask costs warehouse space and tied-up money, and some of the whisky evaporates each year, so older whisky is both rarer and dearer. That is why the price rises faster than the age.

A Three-Bottle Aberlour Flight

A good tasting uses the 12 Year ($44.99), A’bunadh ($69.99) and 16 Year ($89.99), which together cost $204.97. Pour half an ounce of each into matching glasses and taste them in strength order, so start with the two 40% bottles and finish with the cask strength. Taste each neat first and then with a few drops of water.

Note how the sherry shows in each: sweetness, dried fruit and spice. The A’bunadh will taste bigger and hotter, so go slowly. Write down which you prefer, and whether the extra age of the 16 or the extra strength of the A’bunadh appeals more. That decision, between age and strength, is the real choice in this range, and a flight answers it for less than the price of the 18 Year. If you prefer to buy one bottle only, choose the 12 Year if you value gentleness and the A’bunadh if you value intensity, and you can always come back for the others once you know your taste.

How to Taste Sherried Whisky

A sherried malt is easy to enjoy and easy to rush. Use a tulip or Glencairn glass, pour about an ounce and let it sit for a couple of minutes. Nose it gently, because the aromas of dried fruit and spice build over time. Take a first sip neat, hold it briefly, and notice the sweetness first and the spice and oak second. Then add a few drops of water, especially for the cask strength bottle, and taste again.

Serve at room temperature, and keep a glass of water nearby to rest your palate. Store open bottles upright with the cap tight and away from heat and light. Common mistakes are drinking it too cold, which dulls the aroma, adding too much water too quickly, and judging the whisky after one sip. Take your time, and share a pour with a friend so that you can compare notes. If you enjoy the sherried style, keep a small notebook of the bottles you try, with the price, the strength and a line about what you tasted, because after a few months it becomes a personal guide that is more useful than any ranking, and it helps you spot good value the next time you shop.
