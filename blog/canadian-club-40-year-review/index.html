What Is Documented About Canadian Club 40 Year

Official details are thin, so we rely on a named reviewer and flag the gaps. The Rum Howler Blog’s review of Canadian Club 40 Year says the whisky was released to commemorate Canada’s 150th anniversary, with full distribution across Canada as of mid-November in limited quantities, and that it was still available at select locations across Canada at the time of writing. It reports that the whisky aged in Windsor, Ontario, in American oak barrels that had contained 100% corn whisky, and that the barrels were consolidated many times over the 40 years to minimise oxidation of the spirit.

The review does not state the bottling strength, and it does not name the distillery. The brand is owned by Beam Suntory, according to the same page. Those limits matter, because our catalogue lists the bottle at 40% ABV with no age statement field, while other sources describe it differently, so the label is the thing to read.

The Whisky In The 6 film Canadian Club 40 Year Old. Whisky in the 6 #175 is a second independent look at the bottle.

What to Check Before You Buy

Because the documentation is limited, check these points before paying for any Canadian Club 40 Year:

  • The age statement on the label. The name says 40 years, but our catalogue shows no age statement in its data, so confirm the label.
  • The strength. We list 40% ABV, but not every source agrees, so read the bottle and the listing.
  • The release. Canadian Club has issued several older bottlings, so confirm which release you are buying and the packaging.
  • Provenance. A bottle released in limited quantities in 2017 has a history, so ask where it has been.

Anyone planning a canadian club 40 year buy online order should ask all four questions first. If the seller cannot answer these, treat that as information, because a rare bottle is only as good as the paperwork and the honesty behind it, and no amount of marketing can replace a clear answer about what is in the box. For a rare bottle, a clear listing is worth more than a good story.

How Canadian Whisky Age Claims Work

An age statement is easier to trust when you know the rule behind it. The Canadian Food and Drug Regulations, on the Justice Laws page for section B.02.020, say that no claim may be made about a Canadian whisky’s age except the period during which it has been held in small wood. They add that where a whisky has been aged in small wood for at least three years, up to six months in other containers may be claimed as age.

Two practical points follow. First, a 40 year claim on a Canadian whisky refers to time in small wood, so the reported detail of American oak barrels fits the rule. Second, the regulations are updated from time to time, and the page we read shows other whisky sections as repealed, so check the current text if the exact rule matters to you. For a buyer, the point is simple: the age on a Canadian label should describe time in wood, and you can ask the seller to confirm the details for any rare bottle.

Tasting Notes From Reviewers

The Rum Howler Blog reviewer describes butterscotch, oak and vanilla melded into a luscious toffee, followed by fresh corn underlain with firm impressions of tobacco and hints of burlap. Those are one taster’s notes, not the producer’s, and tasting notes vary between tasters and bottles.

If you are curious how a long-aged Canadian whisky compares, a useful approach is to read two or three reviews and look for where they agree. Here, the points of agreement to look for would be oak, vanilla and toffee, which are what you would expect from 40 years in American oak. Aged whisky drinks best neat or with a few drops of water in a tulip glass, at room temperature. Pour a small measure, let it sit for a minute and take your time, because a whisky that spent decades in wood rewards patience more than speed. Store an unopened bottle upright, away from heat and sunlight, and remember that whisky does not improve once it is bottled, so there is no benefit in holding it for longer than you want to.
