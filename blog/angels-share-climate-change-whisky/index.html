Whisky Science

Why Older Whisky Costs More: The Angel's Share, Climate and the Price of Age

By Caskworth Editorial Published September 8, 2026Updated November 7, 20269 min read

Why older whisky costs more comes down to a simple fact: every year a cask sits in a warehouse, part of what is inside evaporates and is gone for good. That loss is called the angel’s share, and it compounds, so a 25 year old whisky comes from a cask that has given up a large fraction of its contents. This guide explains the angel’s share with sourced loss rates, shows how climate changes the picture, puts a price ladder from $39.99 to $999.99 next to the maths, and tells you what to look for when you buy an aged bottle.

What the Angel's Share Is

The angel’s share is the portion of a cask’s contents that evaporates through the wood while the spirit matures. The oak is porous, which is what lets the whisky breathe and take on flavour from the wood, and it is also what lets some of the liquid escape. It is a normal part of maturation, not a fault, and it applies to every cask-aged spirit. Glenfiddich explains the idea in a short film, What is the Angels’ Share?, from the distillery.

One more point from the Scotch Whisky Association is worth knowing: all Scotch must be at least three years old, and a bottle does not keep ageing once it is bottled, so a 12 year old will always remain a 12 year old whisky. That is why the age on the label tells you how long the whisky sat in the cask, which is when the angel’s share takes its toll. The association’s FAQ page sets out these basics.

How Much Whisky Evaporates Each Year

Anyone looking for whisky evaporation explained in numbers will find that published figures vary by climate, warehouse and cask, so treat any single number as an approximation. The trade magazine CLASS, in a November 2025 article on the angel’s share, says Scotch whisky typically loses around 1.5 to 2% a year, bourbon in Kentucky may forfeit 3 to 5% annually, and cognac in the Charente averages 2 to 3%. The same article puts rum in the Caribbean at 8 to 10% or more. You can read it at CLASS: The Angels’ Share explained.

A second source, InsideHook, quotes a distiller putting Scotland at about 2% a year and Kentucky at about 4%, and explains why: Kentucky’s hot days and cool nights make the wood expand and contract, which speeds up how the oak affects the whisky. You can read it in InsideHook’s explainer on whisky and geography.

These are broad averages, and the first question about any bottle is not the average but how long the whisky sat and where.

Why Older Whisky Costs More: The Maths

Evaporation compounds, because each year’s loss is taken from what is left. Using the 2% a year figure for Scotland as a simple illustration (a model, not a measurement of any particular cask), this is how much of the original liquid remains after a given number of years:

Years in caskRemaining at 2% a yearRemaining at 4% a year
12about 78%about 61%
18about 70%about 48%
21about 65%about 42%
25about 60%about 36%
30about 55%about 29%

Read it this way: at 2% a year, a 25 year cask holds only about 60% of what went in, so every bottle of 25 year old whisky comes from liquid that cost 25 years of warehouse space, interest on the money tied up and 40% shrinkage. A 12 year old gets the same cask space for about half as long and loses less. That is the simplest answer to why older whisky costs more, and it is why aged scotch is rare: fewer casks survive to old age in good shape, and each one yields less.

Price also reflects demand and brand, so the maths explains the direction, not the exact amount. Our rare whisky investment guide looks at how rarity feeds into collector prices.

Why Strength Changes in the Cask

Evaporation changes the strength of the whisky as well as the volume. The CLASS article says the loss in Scotland is mainly alcohol, because water stays behind in the humid air. That means a Scotch cask slowly falls in alcoholic strength as well as in volume, so very old Scotch often ends up bottled at modest strengths. You can see it in our own range: the older single malts in the ladder below are listed between 40% and 48% ABV.

This has two practical consequences for buyers. First, a lower strength on an old bottle is normal and is not a sign of dilution. Second, when you compare an aged Scotch with a young cask-strength release, you are comparing two different outcomes of the same process, so look at the strength and the age together. A higher strength is not automatically better, and an older bottle is not automatically stronger.

If you want to taste the effect yourself, pour a small measure of an older bottle and a younger one side by side. The older whisky usually shows more oak, dried fruit and spice, while the younger one shows more grain and fruit. Add a few drops of water to each and notice how the aroma changes. That simple test teaches more than any chart.

Climate, Warehouses and Maturation Speed

Climate changes how fast whisky matures as well as how much is lost. The InsideHook explainer says Scotland’s colder climate slows the process of the oak interacting with the spirit, and that Scotch flagships are typically 10 to 12 years old, while bourbon flagships are typically 4 to 5 years old. That is the basic reason a 21 year old Scotch and a 4 year old bourbon can both be considered fully matured.

Location inside a country matters too. The same article notes that GlenDronach sits in a cooler valley, which suits very long maturation. That is a useful reminder when you shop for aged whisky: a distillery’s microclimate and its warehouse type affect how it ages, so an age statement is not directly comparable across distilleries or countries. If you want to explore this science further, the idea of scotch maturation science comes down to three variables: the wood, the time and the temperature.

What We Can and Cannot Say About Climate Change

A common claim is that a warming climate speeds up evaporation in Scotland and will make very old Scotch scarcer. The sources we reviewed explain how climate affects maturation and loss, but none quantifies a measured effect of climate change on Scotch warehouses, so we do not claim one. What is documented is the mechanism: warmer conditions and bigger temperature swings increase the interaction between spirit and wood and increase evaporation, as the Kentucky comparison shows.

For a buyer, that means two practical points. First, do not assume an age statement means the same thing everywhere, because cool and warm regions mature differently. Second, if a producer or seller makes a specific claim about climate and ageing, ask for the source before you pay for it.

What to Check When You Buy Aged Whisky

When you pay for age, check these things before you order, because the angel’s share means every extra year is paid for in lost liquid:

  1. The age statement and the strength. Compare both with the producer’s own page.
  2. The producer’s description of the casks, because cask type and warehouse affect flavour as much as years.
