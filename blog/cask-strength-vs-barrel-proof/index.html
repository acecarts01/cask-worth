Cask Strength, Barrel Proof and Full Proof Defined

VinePair’s explainer, Barrel Proof, Cask Strength and Full Proof Whiskey Differences Explained, sets out the terms:

  • Barrel proof. Whiskey bottled at the same ABV the liquid has when the distillers dump it from its ageing vessel, with no water added. VinePair says typical strengths fall anywhere between 55 and 60-plus percent ABV.
  • Cask strength. The exact same thing, according to the article. Scotch and Japanese producers tend to favour the term cask strength, while American whiskeys, aged in new charred oak barrels, mostly say barrel proof.
  • Full proof. Usually means bottled at the same proof the spirit had before entering a barrel, as Buffalo Trace uses it. The article says the term is not regulated by the TTB, so producers can use it however they please.

Put simply, cask strength vs barrel proof is mostly a difference of region and habit. Full proof is a different promise, about the strength going in, not coming out. If a label says barrel strength, the article notes that brands may use it for any undiluted whiskey.

What the TTB Rule Allows

VinePair reports that barrel proof whiskeys can differ by a maximum of two degrees in proof, or 1 percent ABV, between dumping and bottling, which it describes as a margin for error and not an allowance for dilution. It adds that multiple barrels from the same run can be blended if the result adheres to the TTB guidelines relating to proof. So a bottle labelled barrel proof should be at or very near the strength it had when emptied from the barrel.

The same article describes the TTB’s own words for full proof as original proof, original barrel proof or entry proof. If you want to read the underlying US rules for whisky, the standards of identity are in 27 CFR 5.143, which cover bourbon, rye and straight whisky, and which set a bottling minimum of 40% ABV.

The practical rule for buyers is this: the term tells you the bottle is undiluted, but it does not tell you how strong the bottle is or how good it is. Always read the strength on the label.

Proof, ABV and What the Numbers Mean

In the United States, proof is twice the alcohol by volume, so 50% ABV is 100 proof. That makes the strengths above easy to convert:

The spread from about 110 to 132 proof matters more than it looks. A bottle at 66.2% has roughly 20% more alcohol per pour than one at 55.1%, so it hits harder and takes more water to be comfortable. That is one reason it makes sense to start with a lower-strength bottle and move up. It is also why strength is worth reading on the label before you buy, because two bottles both called barrel proof can be very different drinks.

To learn what strength does to flavour, taste three bottles in order of strength: Maker’s Mark Cask Strength (55.1%, $64.99), Wild Turkey Rare Breed (58.4%, $39.99) and Larceny Barrel Proof (61%, $49.99). Note how each bottle changes with water: some open up quickly, others need more. Note the heat, the sweetness and the finish. After a few evenings you will know the strength at which you enjoy bourbon most, which is the useful result. If you find them too hot even with water, a standard-strength bourbon is a perfectly good choice, and there is no rule that says high proof is better.

How to Drink Cask Strength Whiskey

You do not have to drink it at full strength. A short routine helps:

  1. Pour a small measure, about an ounce, into a tulip or Glencairn glass.
  2. Nose it gently. Strong spirit can sting the nose, so do not push your face into the glass.
  3. Take a tiny first sip neat to meet the whiskey at its natural strength.
  4. Add water a few drops at a time. Water lowers the strength and often opens up aromas that are hidden at high alcohol. Taste after each addition and stop when you like it.
  5. Or use one large ice cube, which dilutes slowly.

Howcast’s film What Is Cask Strength aka Barrel Proof? shows the basics in a few minutes. Bear in mind that the same whiskey can taste very different at 60% and at 45%, so there is no wrong amount of water as long as you can taste the whiskey.

Serve cask strength whiskey at room temperature and keep a glass of plain water beside you, so you can rest your palate between sips. Take your time with the first pour, because high proof whiskey rewards patience, and avoid mixing it into a cocktail with strong ingredients, which waste the character you paid for. If you do want a cocktail, use the bottle in a drink that is stirred with ice, so the dilution is controlled and the whiskey stays in front.
