Macallan 50 Price: The Lalique Decanter, Its Documented Facts and the Range Below It

By Caskworth Editorial Published September 15, 2026Updated November 9, 20269 min read

The Macallan 50 price at Caskworth is $39,999.99 for the 50 Year in a Lalique crystal decanter, which our catalogue lists at 42.8% ABV. An auction record for the original Six Pillars release gives different figures: 46% ABV, sherry casks and one of 470 numbered decanters, bottled in 2005. This guide sets out what is documented, flags the strength conflict, shows the price per pour for the whole ladder from the 18 Year to the 81 Year, and says what to check before you buy any bottle at this level.

What the Lalique 50 Year Is

The Macallan 50 year old in Lalique crystal belongs to the Six Pillars Collection, a series made with the French crystal house Lalique. Wikipedia’s article on The Macallan distillery says the Six Pillars Collection is bottled in Lalique decanters, and lists the first release as a 50 year old in 2005, limited to 470 units, followed by 55, 57, 60, 62 and 65 year releases. It adds that the distillery was founded in 1824 by Alexander Reid in Craigellachie and has been owned by Edrington since 1999.

A Scotch Whisky Auctions record for the 50 Year Old Lalique describes it as one of 470 individually numbered decanters, 46% ABV, 75 cl, from sherry casks, released in 2005. It says the first Pillar celebrated the oak casks behind the distillery’s reputation, and that Lalique drew on the casks for the decanter, with etched designs in the Art Nouveau style. The lot is also a reminder that unusual bottles do not always sell: that listing failed to meet its reserve.

Anyone searching for a 50 year old Macallan will find several bottles with that age, including a later release that the distillery itself shows in a film titled The Macallan 50 Years Old – 2018 Release. So the exact release matters. The official Macallan channel’s film The Macallan in Lalique Legacy Collection shows the Lalique series in context.

The Strength and Cask Conflict

Our catalogue lists the bottle at 42.8% ABV and describes sherry-seasoned European and American oak. The auction record gives 46% and sherry casks. We could not reach Macallan’s own product page to settle it, so we cannot say which figure is right for the bottle in stock. A 3.2 point difference is large for a bottle at this price, so ask for a photo of the label before you pay, and compare the strength, release year and decanter number with the records above.

The same caution applies to the older bottles. Our catalogue lists the 25, 30 and 40 Year as Double Cask, and range names can differ from the maker's own, so check the label on any bottle you receive for the cask description. Another figure to treat with care is the often-quoted loss of 30 to 40 percent of a cask over fifty years: we could not verify it, so we do not repeat it.

Because the Lalique series is limited and numbered, two bottles of the same age can differ in strength, year and packaging. That is why a documented release matters more than a general description.

The Macallan 50 Year in Lalique Crystal is the main subject here, but it sits within a long ladder. The 81 Year in our catalogue, The Reach, is $49,999.99 and 42.5%. Other Lalique and Rare Macallan Bottles

Wikipedia says a 72 year old Macallan was released in 2018 in the Lalique Genesis Crystal Decanter, limited to 600 units at 42% ABV, which is why searches for a Macallan 72 appear alongside the 50. It also lists a 64 year old in a Lalique decanter that sold for $460,000 at Sotheby’s New York in 2010, with proceeds going to charity: water. We do not list a Macallan 72, and our catalogue lists the 81 Year at $49,999.99 without further release details, so check any bottle against the maker’s own information.

Macallan also sells ranges that are not covered here. The Macallan Harmony Collection is one, and the Rare Cask line is another, and neither is an age-stated Lalique bottle. We have kept this guide to the age ladder so the prices are comparable. For the history of auction records, see our most expensive whisky auction records guide.

How to Taste and Store an Old Single Malt

An old single malt at around 43% rewards patience. Pour about half an ounce into a tulip glass and let it sit for several minutes. Nose it gently, from a distance first, and note what you find before you taste. Old sherry-cask malts often show dried fruit, wood and spice, but notes vary by bottle, so we do not promise any, and we have not poured the 50 Year ourselves.

Take a small first sip and hold it on the tongue. At this strength, a single drop of water may open the aroma, but add very little, because an old whisky can turn thin quickly. Serve it at room temperature, away from strong food, and keep a glass of water nearby. Many owners pour a small amount and share it, because a bottle at this price is rarely finished alone.

Store sealed bottles upright, away from heat and light, and keep the box and decanter paperwork. Whisky does not mature further once bottled, as the Scotch Whisky Association points out, so a sealed bottle will still taste as it did when it was filled. An opened decanter changes slowly with air, so finish it within a sensible period if flavour matters more to you than display.

Mistakes to Avoid

Buyers of ultra-aged whisky repeat the same few errors:

  • Trusting one strength figure. Sources differ, so check the label.
  • Mixing up releases. A 50 Year from 2005 is not the same as a later 50 Year.
  • Skipping provenance. Rare bottles attract fakes, so ask where the bottle came from.
  • Buying on investment claims. Prices can fall, and costs, storage and fees reduce any gain.
  • Ignoring the ladder. A lower bottle, such as the 18 Year, shows the house style for far less.

One more point is expectation. A bottle at $39,999.99 is a purchase for enjoyment, display or collecting, not a promise of future value. Buy it because you want to own it, and keep the receipt and any certificate. If you are gifting it, make sure the recipient is 21 or older and appreciates whisky, since a bottle like this is not a casual present.
