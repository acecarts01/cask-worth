What Cask Finishing Means

Finishing means moving whiskey that has already matured into a second cask for a further period, so the second cask adds flavour. The usual order is a first maturation in charred new oak, as the federal rules for bourbon require, and then a second stage in another barrel such as port, rum, sherry or a specially toasted oak.

The rules for what you may call the whiskey are set out in the US standards at 27 CFR 5.143. That page covers the base definitions for bourbon, rye and straight whisky. A label that says finished tells you a second cask was used, but it does not tell you how long, which cask or how good the base whiskey was, which is why this cask finished bourbon guide spends time on how to judge a bottle yourself.

Finishing is popular because it adds variety to a familiar base. It also has critics, who ask whether a strong finish improves good whiskey or covers weak spirit. Both can be true of different bottles, so judge each one on its own.

How to Judge a Finished Bottle

Use these checks before you pay a premium for the finish:

  1. Ask what the base whiskey is. A producer confident in its spirit usually says so.
  2. Look for specifics. The finishing cask type and the time in it are the details that separate a clear label from vague language.
  3. Taste for balance. A good finish complements the base, and a bad one overwhelms it.
  4. Compare with the unfinished version. Try the finished bottle next to a standard bourbon from the same brand to see what the finish added.
  5. Check strength and price. Finished bottles in this guide run from 43% to 50% ABV and $29.99 to $79.99.

A simple test: pour the finished bourbon and a plain bourbon side by side, noting what changes in sweetness, spice and finish. If you can name the difference and you like it, the finish is working for you. If you cannot tell the two apart, you have saved yourself the premium, which is a perfectly good result. Remember that price and strength matter too: a finished bottle at 47% ABV will taste bolder than one at 43% even before the finish is considered, so compare like with like when you can, and keep notes so you can find the bottle again when you want to buy it a second time, which saves money over many purchases.

Sweet Mash vs Sour Mash Bourbon

The second process question is about the mash. Whisky Advocate defines it simply: whiskeys made without backset are sweet mash whiskeys, and those with backset are sour mash whiskeys, where backset is the liquid strained from the previous batch. The article says backset protects against bacterial contamination during fermentation and supports consistency of flavour, and that sour mash whiskeys are by far the more prevalent style used by major distillers. You can read it in What Are The Differences Between Sweet and Sour Mash Whiskeys?.

On flavour, the same article says sweet mashes give distillers complete control over the flavours imparted from fermentation, while sour mash whiskeys carry the DNA from the prior batch. One distiller it quotes describes sweet mash whiskey as having a softer taste. The word sour describes the mash going into fermentation, not how the whiskey tastes. For a visual explainer, Whiskey & Wine’s video Sour vs Sweet Mash Explained covers the history and science.

In short, the sour mash bourbon process is the norm, and sweet mash is a deliberate choice by some producers. If you see sour mash on a label, it tells you the standard process was used, not that the bourbon is special.

Who Makes Sweet Mash Bourbon

Distiller’s article on sweet mash bourbon says the vast majority of distilleries produce great whiskey using a sour mash technique, and calls Kentucky sweet mash bourbon unusual. It names Wilderness Trail Distillery as at the vanguard of Kentucky’s sweet mash revival, with Kentucky Peerless Distilling and Castle & Key Distillery as two other producers using sweet mash fermentation. Whisky Advocate also names Indiana-based Hard Truth in its list. You can read the details in Distiller’s sweet mash bourbon article.

The producers it quotes are open about the trade-offs. One says a sweet mash leaves you open to more contamination issues and losing batch-to-batch continuity, and another says it is vulnerable in its early stages and needs close attention to cleaning. On flavour, one says the distillate can taste sweeter because it is less acidic, so the natural grain flavours show, while another says sweet mashing results in a cleaner, sweeter distiller’s beer. These are producers’ own views, so treat them as claims to taste for yourself.

A Finish Tasting Flight

The clearest way to learn what a finish does is a controlled tasting. Buy three finished bottles and one plain bourbon as a control: Angel’s Envy ($44.99), Elijah Craig Toasted Barrel ($44.99), Woodford Reserve Double Oaked ($49.99) and Buffalo Trace Bourbon ($28.99), which together cost $168.96. The plain bottle is your reference point, so you can tell what the finishes add.

Pour about half an ounce of each into matching glasses and taste in this order: the plain bourbon first, then the finished ones from lightest to strongest. Write down sweetness, spice, oak, fruit and finish for each. Add a few drops of water and taste again, because water often shows what is underneath. The goal is not to pick a winner, but to learn which finish style you prefer, whether the softer port style, the toasted oak or the double oak, so that your next purchase is a choice and not a guess.

Two practical notes. First, rest your palate with water between samples. Second, do not judge on one visit: come back to the same glasses the next day, and you will often notice things that your first tasting missed.

Transparency: What a Label Should Tell You

Bourbon labels vary in how much they say. A clear label tells you the style, age if any, strength, where it was made and any finish. The more a producer explains, the easier it is to compare bottles. Phrases such as grain to glass bourbon, bourbon terroir and craft sourcing transparency describe producers who say where the grain came from and how the whiskey was made.

Be cautious of vague language. Craft is a producer term with no legal definition that we can point to, and terroir is a claim that a place shapes the whiskey, which is debated for whiskey and not settled. When a label gives a name, a place and a process, you can check it against the producer’s own page. When it does not, you are relying on the story, so judge by the taste and the price.
