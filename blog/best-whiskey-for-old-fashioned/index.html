Best Whiskey for Old Fashioned Cocktails: Bourbon and Rye Picks With Cost Per Drink

By Caskworth Editorial Published August 18, 2026Updated November 9, 20269 min read

The best whiskey for old fashioned cocktails is a bourbon or rye at 45% ABV or higher, because the drink is only whiskey, sugar, bitters and ice, so the bottle does all the work. This guide gives the International Bartenders Association recipe, compares eight bourbons and ryes from $24.99 to $44.99 with strength and cost per drink, explains how to choose between bourbon and rye, and tells you which bottle suits a quick weeknight drink and which suits a dinner party.

The Old Fashioned Recipe

The International Bartenders Association (IBA) lists the Old Fashioned as 45 ml of bourbon or rye whiskey, one sugar cube, a few dashes of Angostura bitters and a few dashes of plain water. The method is to put the sugar cube in an old fashioned glass and soak it with bitters, add a little water and muddle until the sugar dissolves, fill the glass with ice, add the whiskey and stir gently. The garnish is an orange slice or zest and a cocktail cherry. You can read it at the IBA Old Fashioned page.

That is the whole drink, and note that the IBA recipe allows bourbon or rye, so there is no single correct whiskey. The origin of the drink is disputed: the Pendennis Club in Louisville claims it, but cocktail historians reject that claim, so we do not repeat it as fact. For a visual walk-through, Liquor.com’s film How to Make an Old Fashioned Cocktail covers the steps.

What Makes a Whiskey Good for an Old Fashioned

With only four ingredients, a few traits matter. These are practical rules of thumb, not laws:

  • Strength. Ice melts as you stir, so a whiskey at 45% or higher keeps its flavour once diluted. All eight bottles below are at 45% or higher except one.
  • Sweetness that works with the sugar. Bourbon, made from at least 51% corn, tends to give vanilla and caramel that sit well with sugar.
  • Spice that survives the bitters. Rye, made from at least 51% rye, brings pepper and dryness that stand up to Angostura.
  • Price that suits repeat use. You make several drinks from a bottle, so a fair price matters more than a rare label.

The mash rules are set in 27 CFR 5.143. If you are new to the two styles, our bourbon vs whiskey guide explains the difference.

Rye Old Fashioned

A rye Old Fashioned swaps the sweetness of bourbon for pepper and spice, and many people prefer it. In the table, the ryes are Rittenhouse at $29.99 and 50%, Bulleit Rye at $29.99 and 45%, and Michter’s US*1 Rye at $44.99 and 42.4%. For a rittenhouse rye old fashioned buy, our catalogue lists it as a 4 year American rye at 50%, which makes it the strongest of the three.

The simplest way to decide between bourbon and rye is to make one of each and compare. Use the same sugar and bitters, stir the same time and taste side by side. Most people find one clearly preferable, and then you know which bottle to buy again. If you want more rye options, see our rye whiskey collection.

The Maths for a Party

The IBA measure of 45 ml means a 750 ml bottle makes about 16 drinks. For a party, that gives a simple rule: two drinks each for four guests is eight drinks, which is half a bottle, and ten drinks is a bit more than half. At $28.99 a bottle for Buffalo Trace, the whiskey for eight drinks costs about $14.50, and at $37.99 for Eagle Rare it is about $19.

Prepare the sugar and bitters in advance, keep ice ready and make each drink to order, because a stirred drink is best fresh. If you plan to serve a crowd, buy one bourbon and one rye and let guests choose, which makes a good conversation and shows how different the two are. Check the strength of each bottle first, since a 50% bottle stirs differently from a 45% one, and a long stir dilutes both.

A Three-Bottle Old Fashioned Test

To find your house whiskey, make the same Old Fashioned with three bottles: Wild Turkey 101 ($24.99), Buffalo Trace ($28.99) and Bulleit Rye ($29.99), which together cost $83.97. Use the IBA recipe each time, with the same sugar, bitters and stirring time, and taste the three drinks side by side without looking at the labels if a friend can pour for you.

Note which drink is sweeter, which is spicier and which has the longest finish. The point is not to find a winner for everyone, but to find the bottle you would happily make again, and that is a better guide than any list. Then buy a second bottle from the group you liked, such as the Knob Creek 9 Year if you enjoyed the high-proof bourbon, or the Rittenhouse if you liked the rye, and repeat the test. Two rounds will usually settle it.

How to Choose

A simple guide:

Mistakes to Avoid

A few things spoil an Old Fashioned more often than the choice of whiskey:

  • Using a very light whiskey. A low-strength or delicate bottle disappears under ice and bitters.
  • Over-stirring. The IBA method says stir gently. Too much dilution makes the drink thin.
  • Using a rare bottle. A collectible or very old whiskey is wasted in a cocktail. Save it for sipping.
  • Skipping the garnish. The orange zest adds aroma, and the IBA lists it, with a cocktail cherry.

Two small habits help as well. Use the largest, clearest ice you can, because big cubes melt slowly and keep the drink cold without turning it watery, and chill the glass if you have time. Taste the drink before you garnish, and adjust the next one by a dash of bitters or a little more sugar until it suits you, since the IBA recipe is a starting point and personal taste decides the rest. Keep notes on what you changed, so that your best version is easy to repeat for friends, and so that the next bottle you buy is a considered choice, not a guess, and so that the drink you serve is always one you are happy with.
