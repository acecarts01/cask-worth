Whiskey Basics

Bourbon vs Whiskey: What the Law Says, How They Taste and What to Buy

By Caskworth Editorial Published October 16, 2026Updated November 7, 20269 min read

Bourbon is a whiskey, but not every whiskey is bourbon. In the bourbon vs whiskey question, “whiskey” is the broad category of spirits distilled from fermented grain and aged in wood, while bourbon is one American style inside it with strict legal rules about its grain, its barrels and its strength. This guide sets out those rules, compares bourbon with the other major styles, explains why two bourbons can taste so different, and points you to starter bottles from $22.99 so you can taste the differences yourself.

Bourbon vs Whiskey: The Short Answer

Think of whiskey as the family and bourbon as one member of it. Scotch, Irish whiskey, rye, Tennessee whiskey, Canadian whisky and Japanese whisky are all whiskey too. What sets a bourbon apart is a short list of rules written into United States federal regulations, which a bottle must meet before the word “bourbon” may appear on the label.

That means a bottle that just says “whiskey” tells you very little. It could be a blended Canadian whisky, a Scotch single malt or a Kentucky bourbon. The style name (bourbon, rye, Scotch) is what tells you what is inside, and the rest of this guide explains how to read it.

The relationship is similar to the one between cognac and brandy, where one is a protected, rule-bound subset of the other.

The Legal Rules for Bourbon

The standards of identity for American whisky are set out in federal regulations. The text of 27 CFR 5.143 on whisky standards of identity defines bourbon, rye and straight whisky, and it is the source for the points below.

  • Made in the United States. The regulation says “bourbon” may not describe any whisky not distilled and aged in the United States.
  • At least 51% corn. The fermented grain mash must be at least 51% corn. The rest is usually rye or wheat plus malted barley.
  • Distilled to no more than 160 proof (80% alcohol by volume), so that grain flavour carries into the spirit.
  • Stored in charred new oak barrels at no more than 125 proof (62.5% ABV) when it goes in.
  • Bottled at 80 proof (40% ABV) or higher.
  • Nothing added but water. Bourbon cannot be flavoured or coloured, which is why bourbon colour comes entirely from the wood.

Notice that the regulation does not set a minimum age for plain bourbon. The two-year minimum belongs to straight whisky: straight whisky is stored in charred new oak barrels for at least two years. A bourbon that meets that extra rule is called straight bourbon, and most of the better-known bottles on the shelf are straight bourbons, even when the front label does not make a point of it.

Mash Bills and Why Bourbons Taste Different

The law fixes only a minimum for corn, so each distillery picks its own recipe, called a mash bill. This is why a single legal category covers such a wide range of flavour.

  • Standard bourbon mash bills use mostly corn with a modest share of rye or wheat and some malted barley, giving a balance of sweetness and spice.
  • High rye bourbon whiskey uses a bigger portion of rye, which adds pepper and a drier edge. Bulleit Bourbon is sold in this style and is listed in our catalogue as a high-rye Kentucky bourbon at 45% ABV.
  • Wheated bourbon swaps rye for wheat, giving a softer, rounder, sweeter whiskey.

Barrel handling matters as much as the grain. Because the barrels are new and charred, every bourbon picks up vanilla, caramel and toasted oak from the wood, but how long it stays there, and in which warehouse, changes the result. An age statement is therefore a clue to flavour, not a guarantee of quality: the Eagle Rare 10 Year is a 10 year single barrel at 45% ABV, while Buffalo Trace Bourbon carries no age statement at the same strength.

A beginner can think of it in three steps: grain gives the backbone (sweet corn, peppery rye or soft wheat), new charred oak gives the vanilla and caramel, and time in the barrel rounds off the edges.

Bourbon vs Rye Whiskey

People often ask about bourbon vs rye whiskey, because both are American, both use new charred oak and both can look similar on a shelf. The difference is the main grain. Bourbon must be at least 51% corn. Rye whiskey must be at least 51% rye. Corn gives sweetness and body, while rye gives spice, dryness and a peppery bite.

In the glass, the rye vs bourbon contrast is easy to taste. Bourbon leans towards vanilla, caramel and brown sugar. Rye leans towards black pepper, mint, dried herbs and a longer, drier finish. That is why rye is a favourite in a Manhattan, while bourbon is common in an Old Fashioned and neat.

Kentucky, Straight and Other Label Terms

Many buyers think bourbon has to be made in Kentucky. It does not. The regulation requires only that bourbon be made in the United States. Kentucky is its heartland and makes most of it, but if a label names a State, the regulation says that State may be used only if the distillation and any required aging took place there. For example, a New York bourbon must be distilled and aged in New York.

That is why a label reading bourbon whiskey Kentucky straight carries more information than the plain word bourbon: it tells you the whiskey is straight, meaning it was stored in charred new oak barrels for at least two years, and it names Kentucky as the place. When people search for bourbon whiskey Kentucky straight, they are usually asking for exactly that kind of bottle. Buffalo Trace Bourbon is listed in our catalogue as a Kentucky straight bourbon at $28.99.

These terms matter when you compare bourbon brands, because two bottles at similar prices can differ in age, proof and style. The best way to compare is to look at the style, the age statement and the strength together, then taste.

Whisky or Whiskey?

Both spellings mean the same thing. The usual convention is that the United States and Ireland write “whiskey”, while Scotland, Canada and Japan write “whisky”. A few American brands choose the shorter spelling. The spelling says nothing about quality or style, so rely on the style name instead. The federal regulation itself uses “whisky”, even for bourbon.

On Caskworth you will see both: “whiskey” for American and Irish bottles, and “whisky” where the producer uses it.
