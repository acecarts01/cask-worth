What Calvados Is

Calvados is the apple brandy of Normandy, in northern France, made from fermented apple cider with pears allowed in some appellations and aged in French oak. Where cognac and Armagnac start from grape wine, Calvados starts from apples, which is why it is often called Normandy apple brandy. Difford’s Guide says the cider is distilled to roughly 68 to 72% alcohol by volume and that more than 120 apple varieties are permitted, grouped into bitter, tart, sweet and bittersweet types.

The spirit must be aged at least two years in French oak, according to the same guide, which you can read in its Calvados entry. For a short film overview, Inside World-Spirits TV has Calvados Explained: Apple, Pear & Wood. If you want the American side of apple brandy, our apple brandy and applejack guide compares the two.

The Three Appellations

Difford’s Guide describes three appellations, and the differences are real:

AppellationPearsDistillationNotes from Difford’s
AOC CalvadosNo limit on the share of pearsColumn or potCovers much of Basse-Normandie and over 70% of production
AOC Pays d’AugeUp to 30% pearsDouble distillation in pot stillsConsidered the finest tier, with at least six weeks of fermentation
AOC DomfrontaisAt least 30% perry pearsSingle column onlyPot distillation is banned

In practice, a bottle labelled Pays d’Auge was made in pot stills and is often described as the more traditional style, while a plain Calvados may use a column still. Domfrontais is the pear-led style. If you see Calvados Domfrontais or Pays d’Auge on a label, it tells you which rules applied. A film on one of them, What is Calvados Domfrontais?, shows the pear-based style.

Calvados Age Labels Explained

The age on the label is the minimum age of the youngest brandy in the blend. According to Difford’s Guide:

LabelMinimum age
Fine, Trois étoiles or Trois pommes2 years
Vieux or Réserve3 years
VSOP, V.O. or Vieille Réserve4 years
XO, Extra, Napoléon, Hors d’Âge or Âge Inconnu6 years

The guide adds that XO is often as old as 25 years, so the label is a floor. This is a close parallel with cognac, where VSOP is at least 4 years and XO at least 10 years under the BNIC rules. So a Calvados VSOP and a cognac VSOP share the same minimum of 4 years, but a Calvados XO needs only 6 years while a cognac XO needs 10. If you are used to cognac, remember that difference when you compare prices, and see our cognac VSOP vs XO guide for the cognac side.

Calvados vs Cognac

The two are both brandies aged in oak, but the raw material differs. Cognac is made from grape wine in the Cognac region, while Calvados is made from apple cider, sometimes with pear, in Normandy. That shows in the glass: Calvados tends to be fruitier and more orchard-led, while cognac tends to show grape and floral notes. Neither is better. They are different traditions, and a Calvados is a useful addition if you already enjoy cognac and want to explore. If you are comparing calvados vs cognac on price, remember that the cheapest Calvados here is $39.99 while cognac VSOPs in our range start around $50, so apple brandy is an affordable way to try a new style of aged spirit, which is a good reason to taste it side by side with a cognac you already know.

The minimum-age rules also differ, as the age table shows. For a cognac buyer, a Calvados VSOP at $39.99 to $44.99 is a low-cost way to try the style, while an XO at $99.99 gives a longer-aged expression.

How to Read a Calvados Label

A Calvados label can carry four useful pieces of information, and each one tells you something:

  • The appellation. Calvados, Calvados Pays d’Auge or Calvados Domfrontais, which tell you the production rules that applied, such as pot stills for Pays d’Auge and at least 30% perry pears for Domfrontais.
  • The age label. Fine, Vieux, VSOP or XO and their equivalents, which set a minimum age for the youngest brandy in the blend.
  • The strength. Most bottles in our range are 40% to 42%, in line with other brandies.
  • The producer. The producer’s own page explains the apples, the stills and the ageing, which is where details beyond the label belong.

If a label gives only Calvados with no appellation or age, treat it as a basic bottle and judge it on taste and price. If it gives all four, you can compare it properly with another bottle, which is the point of the system.

A Three-Bottle Calvados Flight

The best way to learn Calvados is to taste across the age ladder. A simple flight is Busnel VSOP ($39.99), Christian Drouin VSOP ($44.99) and Château du Breuil XO ($99.99), which together cost $184.97. Pour about half an ounce of each, taste in price order, and add a few drops of water to each to see how the aroma opens.

Note the apple character first, then the oak, then the finish. The two VSOPs show how two producers handle the same minimum age, and the XO shows what extra time adds. If you cannot tell the XO from the VSOP, buy the VSOP and keep the difference, and if you can, you have found a style worth exploring further.

Common mistakes to avoid are serving it too cold, which hides the aroma, using a tiny glass and judging a bottle after one sip. Give each pour a minute to settle and taste it twice.

How to Drink Calvados

Calvados is flexible. Younger bottles such as a Fine or VSOP work well in cocktails and in long drinks with tonic or ginger beer, while older bottles are best sipped neat at room temperature after a meal. Pour a small measure into a tulip glass, let it sit for a minute and notice the apple and the oak. Add a few drops of water if you want to open the aroma.

Store the bottle upright, away from heat and light. Like other aged spirits, Calvados does not improve in the bottle, so open it when you want to enjoy it.
