How to Buy Real Japanese Whisky: Labelling Rules and Why Many Bottles Have No Age Statement

By Caskworth Editorial Published September 8, 2026Updated November 9, 20269 min read

Knowing how to buy real Japanese whisky starts with the label, because for years the words Japanese whisky had no firm legal meaning. This guide explains the industry standard from the Japan Spirits & Liqueurs Makers Association, which the House of Suntory says applies to bottles labelled and bottled after 1 April 2024, why many bottles carry no age statement, what Suntory says about Toki, how a 2018 stock shortage pushed producers towards no-age-statement releases, and ten Japanese whiskies in our range from $34.99 to $149.99 with price per pour.

What Counts as Japanese Whisky

The House of Suntory’s page on the Japanese whisky definition sets out the standards of the Japan Spirits & Liqueurs Makers Association (JSLMA). It says they apply to all whisky labelled and bottled after 1 April 2024 by JSLMA members. Under the standards:

  • Raw materials: malted grain is required, and other cereal grains may be used, with water extracted in Japan.
  • Production: saccharification, fermentation and distillation must take place at a distillery in Japan, with distillation at less than 95% ABV.
  • Ageing: at least three years in wooden casks, in Japan.
  • Bottling: in Japan, at no less than 40% ABV.
  • Colouring: plain, unflavoured caramel only.

The page adds that producers may not use misleading marketing on whisky that fails the standards, such as a Japanese person’s name, a place name, a scenic spot or the Japanese flag. That is the core of how to buy real japanese whisky: look for a producer that follows the rules, and be careful of a label that leans on Japanese imagery without a clear producer. Note that this is an industry association standard that binds its members, not a government law. The video Whisky Stuff 3 - JSLMA Japanese Whisky Regulations 2021 from Whisky Lock gives a viewer’s summary of the earlier announcement.

Why Many Japanese Whiskies Have No Age Statement

A no age statement bottle, often shortened to NAS, simply does not print an age, and this is the heart of NAS Japanese whisky explained in one line. The JSLMA definition above sets a three-year minimum for Japanese whisky, and the Suntory page we read says nothing that would require an age to be printed. That is why Japanese whisky no age statement is so common: the standard sets a minimum, not a number on the label.

There is a practical reason for producers to do it. A blend without an age statement lets the blender choose whiskies of different ages to reach a flavour, without having to meet one stated age across the whole bottle. That is the producer’s freedom, and it is true of Scotch as well. It does not mean the whisky is better or worse. Judge a NAS bottle on the producer, the strength, the price and the taste, as you would any other.

If you want to read about the age rules for Scotch, the Scotch Whisky Association says all Scotch must be at least three years old, as set out in its FAQ. The minimum age is the same three years, which is a useful comparison. Our Japanese whisky vs Scotch guide compares the two in more detail.

Suntory Toki: What the Producer Says

A good example of a NAS bottle is Suntory Toki. The House of Suntory describes it as a vivid blend of malt and grain whisky from Suntory’s three iconic distilleries, and names three components: Hakushu lightly peated for vibrant, bright orchard fruits, green herbal notes and gentle smoke; Chita heavy grain for rich grains, soft sweetness and a rounded texture; and Yamazaki Spanish oak cask for Japanese spices and incense. The page calls it a subtle, refined yet complex whisky and recommends a chilled Japanese highball.

Notice what the page does not give: an age or a strength, which is why searches for suntory toki age have no single answer from the producer. Our catalogue lists the Suntory Toki at $34.99 and 43% ABV, which you should confirm on the label. For the Hibiki range, the same idea applies: a hibiki no age statement bottle such as Harmony is a blend, and our catalogue lists Hibiki Harmony at $79.99 and 43%.

The 2018 Shortage and Age Statements

Age statements are also a supply question. In May 2018, Suntory announced it would suspend sales of Hakushu 12 and Hibiki 17, because demand had exceeded supply and left a stock shortage, according to coverage including Time Out Tokyo. Aged stock takes years to replace, so a producer that cannot make more 12 or 17 year old whisky has a reason to release blends without an age statement.

That is the history behind the question of japanese whisky shortage explained: popularity rose faster than aged stock. It does not mean age-stated bottles are gone, and our range still includes the Hakushu 12 Year at $149.99. It does mean that availability can change, so check the product page before you order.

Blended, Single Malt and Grain Whisky

Japanese labels use several style words, and knowing them helps you compare bottles. In our catalogue you will see:

  • Blended Japanese, such as Suntory Toki and Hibiki Harmony, which combine different whiskies from different stills. The Toki page names malt and grain components from three distilleries.
  • Single malt, such as Akashi, Fuji, Miyagikyo and Hakushu 12 Year, which come from one distillery’s malt whisky.
  • Blended malt, such as Nikka Taketsuru Pure Malt, which combines malt whiskies from more than one place.
  • Grain whisky, such as Nikka Coffey Whisky, which is made from grain and tends to be lighter in style than a malt.

The style tells you what to expect: a blend is usually the most approachable, a single malt shows a distillery’s character, and a grain whisky is the lightest. None of these terms tells you the age, which is why NAS is common across all four styles.

How to Serve Japanese Whisky

Suntory’s Toki page recommends a chilled Japanese highball: chill the glass, the whisky and the soda, pay attention to the ice and to how the soda is poured, and add a lemon peel or grapefruit coin for aroma. That method suits the lighter blends and grain whiskies in the table. Higher-strength or malt-led bottles such as Nikka From the Barrel at 51.4% are better neat or with a few drops of water.

If you are trying Japanese whisky for the first time, start with one blend and one single malt, taste them side by side, and note which you prefer. That small step teaches more about the labels above than any list, and it makes your next purchase much easier. Store bottles upright, away from heat and light, and keep the cap tight once a bottle is open. Remember too that a bottle does not keep ageing after it is bottled, so there is no reason to wait to open one, and a simple notebook of what you tried and liked will make future purchases easier and cheaper.

How to Buy Real Japanese Whisky: A Checklist

Use this checklist before you order any bottle sold as Japanese whisky:

  1. Check the producer. Look for a named distillery or company, and read its own page for how the whisky is made.
  2. Look for the words in the standard. Distilled and bottled in Japan, at 40% or more, are the basics.
  3. Be wary of imagery. A label that uses a Japanese name, scene or flag without naming a producer or distillery is a reason to ask questions.
