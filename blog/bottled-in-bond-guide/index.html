What Bottled-in-Bond Requires

The federal rule is 27 CFR 5.88. It says bottled in bond spirits must be the same kind, distilled from the same class of materials, in the same distilling season, by the same distiller, at the same distillery. They must be stored at least four years in wooden containers, with the spirits in contact with the wood, and must not be altered in character. Proofing may only use pure water, and the spirits must be bottled at 50% alcohol by volume, which is 100 proof.

The rule also controls the label. A domestic bottle must name the distillery, or the trade name under which the distiller distilled and warehoused the spirits, and show the distillery’s DSP number and the number of the plant where it was bottled. That makes a bottled-in-bond label more informative than most. For a quick video overview, Fred Minnick’s What Is Bottled in Bond Bourbon? is a good companion.

Put simply, the label tells you the age floor, the strength, the single season and the distillery. This bottled in bond guide uses those four facts as a checklist when comparing bottles.

The 1897 Act and Colonel Taylor

Wikipedia says the Bottled-in-Bond Act is dated 3 March 1897 and was a response to widespread adulteration of American whiskey, which was often flavoured and coloured with iodine, tobacco and other substances. The Act made the federal government the guarantor of a spirit’s authenticity. It also let distillers delay paying excise tax until ageing was complete, with warehouse supervision to make sure the tax was eventually collected.

The same article names Colonel E.H. Taylor Jr., creator of Old Taylor bourbon, as the man who led a group of distillers who joined Treasury Secretary John G. Carlisle to push for the Act. That is why the name appears on modern bonded bottles, and why anyone who searches for an EH Taylor bourbon or even a Colonel EH Taylor buy online result will find bonded releases.

It is worth noting how Wikipedia describes the term today: some consumers see it as a mark of quality, while many producers consider it archaic. We read that as a reminder that the label is a set of facts, not a score.

What the Label Does Not Promise

Bottled in bond is not a taste guarantee. It does not say the whiskey is better, only that it met the rule. The rule as published in 27 CFR 5.88 does not set a mash bill, so bonded bottles can be bourbon, rye or another spirit, and flavour varies widely. It also does not mean the bottle is cheap, rare or old beyond four years.

It is also not the same as a straight bourbon label. Straight whiskey has its own definition, in the bourbon rules at 27 CFR 5.143 for example, and a bottle can be both. Read the label for the class of spirit, then for the bonded statement, then for the age. If you want to learn how the two labels relate, our bourbon vs whiskey guide explains the main rules.

Finally, price does not follow the label. Our catalogue lists bonded bottles from $27.99 to $499.99, so the statement does not set the price. Age, scarcity and demand do.

How to Read a Bonded Label

A bonded bottle packs a lot of information into a small label. Use this list when you pick one up:

  • The bonded statement. Look for the words bottled in bond, which signal that the bottle claims to meet the rule.
  • The distillery. The rule requires the real name of the distillery, or the trade name it used, so you can see who made the spirit.
  • The DSP numbers. The label shows the number of the distillery that made the spirit and of the plant that bottled it. They are two separate numbers, and they may be different.
  • The strength. It should read 50% alcohol by volume, or 100 proof.
  • The age. Bonded means at least four years. A bottle may state more, as the 7 Year and 19 Year above do.
  • The class. Bourbon, rye or another type, because the bond rule applies to all of them.

If any of these is missing or unclear, ask the seller before you buy. A sealed bottle with a clear label is the easiest to verify, and a reputable seller will answer questions about it without hesitation.

A Bonded Tasting Flight

Because every bonded bottle is bottled at the same strength, a bonded flight is a fair comparison. Pick three from the table, for example Jim Beam Bonded 4 Year, Heaven Hill Bottled-in-Bond 7 Year and Rittenhouse Rye, which together cost $102.98. Pour half an ounce of each into matching glasses, nose them gently, and taste from the youngest to the oldest.

At 50% each pour is stronger than a standard 40% bottle, so go slowly and add a few drops of water if the heat covers the flavour. Note how the extra three years in the 7 Year change the texture compared with the 4 Year, and how the rye differs from the two bourbons. Write a line per glass.

The result is a cheap lesson in what age, grain and distillery each add when the strength is held constant. It also tells you whether the higher-priced bonded bottles, such as E.H. Taylor Small Batch at $49.99 or Old Fitzgerald 19 Year at $499.99, are worth chasing for your own taste. Many drinkers find that the four-year bottles already give them most of what they want, and some will want the extra age. Either answer is useful, and the notes you take will make your next purchase easier and keep you from overspending on a label alone.
