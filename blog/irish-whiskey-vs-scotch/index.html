Irish Whiskey vs Scotch Guide: The Rules, the Taste and Seven Bottles to Try

This Irish whiskey vs Scotch guide starts with the rules, because the two differ in more than spelling. Irish Whiskey vs Scotch Guide: The Rules Side by Side

Irish whiskey is defined by the Technical File that the Irish government submitted for its geographical indication, and the UK government hosts a copy as the Irish whiskey specification. It says Irish whiskey is distilled on the island of Ireland, including Northern Ireland, from a mash of malted cereals, with or without whole grains of other cereals, distilled at less than 94.8% by volume, and matured for at least three years in wooden casks not exceeding 700 litres. Only water and plain caramel colouring may be added, and the minimum bottled strength is 40%.

The Scotch Whisky Association states that Scotch must be matured in oak casks for at least three years and bottled at no less than 40% ABV, as set out in its FAQ. The Irish file points out one difference in wood: Irish whiskey may use wooden casks, while Scotch must be matured in oak.

Triple Distillation and Pot Stills

The Technical File says the traditional practice is to triple distil Irish whiskey, which produces a lighter and smoother spirit, and notes that the majority of Scottish and American distilleries double distil. That is a tendency, not a rule: the file calls triple distillation traditional, not compulsory, and some Irish whiskey is not triple distilled.

Ireland also has a category with no exact Scottish equivalent. According to the file, pot still Irish whiskey uses both unmalted and malted barley, and the unmalted barley gives a distinctive spicy flavour and a creamy mouth-feel, while malt Irish whiskey uses 100% malted barley and has a fruity, floral and malty character. Anyone looking for a single pot still Irish buy should look for the words pot still on the label. For a short film on the style, watch What is Single Pot Still Irish Whiskey.

Single pot still is different from single malt, which is the Scottish category made from malted barley at one distillery. So single pot still vs single malt is really a question about the grain bill, not just the country. Our Redbreast and pot still guide covers the style in detail.

Peat and Flavour

Peat is the best-known flavour split. The Technical File says Irish whiskey is produced using unpeated malted barley, although some peated malted barley is used in the industry, so Irish whiskey peat is the exception, not the rule. Scotch includes many peated styles, especially from Islay, and many unpeated ones, from Speyside for example.

That is why Irish whiskey often tastes softer and fruitier to new drinkers, while Scotch has the wider range, from sweet and fruity to smoky and medicinal. Neither is better; they are different traditions. People often ask for an Irish whiskey for Scotch drinkers, and the best answer is a pot still bottle such as Redbreast 12 Year at $64.99 and 40% ABV, because its texture and spice differ from any Scotch.

If you are used to Scotch, expect less smoke and a creamier body from the pot still style. If you are used to Irish, a Speyside single malt is the gentlest way into Scotch, and Islay is the bold end.

How to Taste Them Side by Side

A side-by-side tasting is the cheapest way to learn the difference. Pick one Irish pot still and one Scotch single malt, for example Green Spot and Glenfiddich 12, and pour half an ounce of each into matching glasses. Nose each gently, because a hard sniff of 40% spirit registers mostly alcohol. Taste the Irish first, since it is usually lighter, then the Scotch, and add a few drops of water to each.

Write one line per glass. Many drinkers note creamy texture and fruit in the pot still, and a drier, maltier or fruitier profile in the Speyside malt. If you add a peated bottle such as Laphroaig 10, taste it last, because its smoke will overpower anything after it. Keep a glass of water nearby and wait a minute between pours. Rinse the glasses between rounds so earlier flavours do not carry over into the next pour.

Do not judge on one sip. The first taste mostly shows strength, and the second and third show flavour. Take your time, and keep notes, because they will guide your next purchase.
