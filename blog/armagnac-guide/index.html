Buy Armagnac Online: Age Labels, Vintages and the Bottles to Start With

If you want to buy Armagnac online, the labels follow age rules that are easy to learn: VS means at least one year, VSOP at least four, XO at least ten since April 2018, and a vintage comes from grapes harvested in one stated year. This Armagnac guide sets out those rules from the Armagnac trade body and trade reporting, explains Armagnac vs cognac, and compares eleven bottles in our range from $49.99 to $229.99 by price and price per pour, so you can choose a first bottle with confidence.

What Armagnac Is

Armagnac is a brandy from south-west France. The Armagnac trade body says it has been made there for more than 700 years, and lists the appellation zones as Bas-Armagnac, Armagnac-Ténarèze and Haut-Armagnac. Its website, armagnac.fr, describes the spirit ageing slowly in cellars in oak casks. Many of the bottles in our range say Bas Armagnac or Bas-Armagnac, which is one of those zones, so a bas armagnac label tells you where the grapes were grown.

That is the basic definition: a grape brandy with a protected place of origin and its own age rules, which you can read in the next section. For a short film comparing the two famous French brandies, Flaviar’s What’s the Difference Between Cognac and Armagnac? is a good start.

Armagnac Age Labels Explained

The armagnac.fr label guide says age statements refer to the youngest eau-de-vie in the blend, so older spirits may also be present. Its minimums are VS (three stars) at least 1 year, VSOP at least 4 years, Hors d’Âge at least 10 years, and a vintage made only from grapes of one harvest and aged at least 10 years. Difford’s Guide adds that a VS must be at least one year old, and two years if it is to be exported, and that VSOP must be at least four years old.

The XO rule has changed. The armagnac.fr label page still lists XO at 6 years, but Drinks International reported that the minimum for the youngest eau-de-vie in an XO blend rose to 10 years for Armagnac bottled from 1 April 2018, with older stock packaged before that date allowed to be sold until it runs out. You can read it in Armagnac XO age guide increases to 10 years. So an armagnac xo bottled today should be at least 10 years, and an older bottle may follow the old rule.

Vintage Armagnac Explained

Vintage armagnac is a feature of the category. Difford’s Guide says all the Armagnac in a vintage blend must have been harvested in the stated year, and that vintage bottlings may be reduced to 40% but most are bottled at cask strength, typically 40 to 48% ABV. The armagnac.fr page adds that a vintage bottle gives its bottling date, so you can work out exactly how many years it aged, since Armagnac stops ageing once bottled.

That is a useful check: year of harvest plus bottling date equals years in cask. If a label gives only a vintage year with no bottling date, ask for it. Our range includes older vintage bottles at very high prices, and for any of them you should confirm the details on the product page and keep the paperwork.

Armagnac vs Cognac

Both are French grape brandies with protected origins and age labels, which makes armagnac vs cognac a natural question. The age rules are similar in form but not identical: for cognac, the BNIC lists VSOP at 4 years and XO at 10, so VSOP and XO now match Armagnac’s minimums, while VS differs, at 2 years for cognac against 1 for Armagnac. Our cognac VSOP vs XO guide explains the cognac side.

The French brandy guide answer for a buyer is simple: both are worth trying, and a side-by-side tasting of a VSOP from each region shows the difference far better than any description.

Reading the Price Ladder

The two Delord bottles give a clean way to see how age and price relate, because both state their age and share the same listed strength. The 15 Year is $79.99, about $5.33 per year of age, and the 25 Year is $199.99, about $8.00 per year. The 25 costs 2.5 times as much for 1.7 times the age, which is the usual pattern in aged spirits: each extra year costs more than the last.

The XO bottles show a different pattern. Janneau XO is $129.99 and Tariquet XO is $149.99, with the Château de Laubade XO also at $149.99. They sit between the VSOPs at about $50 to $55 and the 25 year old at about $200, so the ladder from VSOP to XO to a long age statement is a sensible way to climb. If you are deciding where to stop, remember that the label is a minimum, so an XO may well be older than ten years, and the producer’s own information is where to check.

A Three-Bottle Armagnac Flight

The best way to learn the category is to taste across it. A sensible flight is Janneau VSOP ($49.99), Tariquet VSOP ($54.99) and Delord 15 Year ($79.99), which together cost $184.97. Pour about half an ounce of each into matching glasses and taste them in price order, neat first and then with a few drops of water.

Notice the differences between the two VSOPs, which share a minimum age but come from different houses, and then notice what the older age statement adds. Write down your impressions, and compare them with your notes on any cognac you already know. Common mistakes to avoid are serving it too cold, using a small glass that hides the aroma, and judging after a single sip. Give each pour a minute, taste it twice, and you will quickly learn which style you prefer and which bottle to buy next. Sharing the flight with a friend makes it more useful, because two sets of notes catch more than one. How to Drink Armagnac

Armagnac is best sipped neat after a meal. Pour a small measure into a tulip glass, let it sit for a minute and nose it before tasting. A few drops of water can open the aroma. Store the bottle upright, away from heat and sunlight, and remember that, as armagnac.fr says, it stops ageing once bottled, so there is no benefit in keeping it unopened.
