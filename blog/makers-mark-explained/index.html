What Wheated Bourbon Means

Most bourbons use rye as the secondary grain after corn. A wheated bourbon uses wheat instead, which usually gives a softer, rounder, sweeter whiskey. Wikipedia’s account of the brand says Maker’s Mark uses red winter wheat (16%), corn (70%) and malted barley (14%), with no rye. Those percentages come from that source, and the distillery’s own pages we could read do not repeat them.

The producer describes its flavour as a softer, sweeter style without the burn and bite, with a signature velvety mouthfeel and notes of bright cherry, delicate vanilla, caramel and a hint of soft spices. It suggests drinking it neat, on the rocks or in a classic Whisky Sour. You can read this on the official Maker’s Mark page, which also notes the brand ranked sixth in Drinks International’s Most Admired Whiskies for 2025. If you want a wheated bourbon whiskey to compare, our wheated bourbon guide covers the style more widely.

The federal definition still applies: bourbon must be at least 51% corn, aged in charred new oak, and bottled with nothing added but water, as set out in 27 CFR 5.143.

Maker's Mark Original: The Benchmark

The Maker’s Mark Original is $29.99 in our catalogue, listed at 45% ABV, and Wikipedia says the standard bottling is 90 proof, which is 45% ABV, so the figures match. It is the bottle with the hand-dipped red wax seal, a look the same source credits to Margie Samuels, the wife of T. William Samuels.

The history is short and well documented: according to Wikipedia, Bill Samuels Sr. bought Burks’ Distillery in Loretto, Kentucky, on 1 October 1953, production began in 1954 and the first bottling was in 1958. The producer says the flavour and quality of its whisky are shaped by the land at its homeplace, Star Hill Farm. For a quick independent view of the bottle, SLB Drinks’ film Is Maker’s Mark Bourbon WORTH The MONEY? covers the value question.

The Full Maker's Mark Range

The producer’s own site lists more bottles than we carry, and knowing the full range helps you read the names. Its range includes Maker’s Mark Kentucky Straight Bourbon (also called Classic), Maker’s Mark 46 French Oaked Bourbon, Cask Strength, 101 Special Proof, a Wood Finishing Series with a Stewards Release, Cellar Aged, Private Selection, and global travel exclusives, plus limited releases such as Greats of the Gate.

That means a name such as Maker’s Mark Original in our catalogue is the same bottle the producer calls Classic or Kentucky Straight Bourbon, so check the label if you compare listings. For any limited release, the producer’s own page is the place for the details, and availability can change from year to year. How to Drink Maker's Mark

The producer suggests serving the Classic bourbon neat, on the rocks or in a classic Whisky Sour, and its site lists mixed drinks including the Maker’s Sour, the Maker’s Collins and a Kentucky Classic Collins, all citrus-led and described as light and bright. A wheated bourbon works well here because it is softer, so the citrus does not fight it.

  • Neat. Pour a small measure, let it sit for a minute and look for the cherry, vanilla and caramel the producer describes.
  • On the rocks. One large cube keeps it cold without watering it down quickly.
  • In a sour or a Collins. Fresh lemon, a little sugar and the bourbon is all you need.
  • Higher strength bottles. At 47% or 55.1%, the 46 and Cask Strength can take more water or a larger cube.

Store the bottle upright, away from heat and sunlight, and keep the cap tight between pours.

A Three-Bottle Tasting Plan

To learn how the range differs, buy Maker’s Mark Original ($29.99), Maker’s 46 ($39.99) and Cask Strength ($64.99), which together cost $134.97. Pour half an ounce of each into matching glasses and taste them in that order. Note how the sweetness changes, how much oak you notice and how hot each one is. Then add a few drops of water to the Cask Strength and taste it again.

Most people discover a clear preference within two or three evenings. If you like the softness of the Original, you may not need the others. If you like the extra wood of the 46, it becomes your everyday bottle. If you enjoy the Cask Strength, you have found a gateway to higher-proof bourbon, and our cask strength guide shows where to go next. Whatever you learn, write it down so you can buy with confidence next time.

Maker's Mark 46: The Seared French Oak Bourbon

Maker’s 46 is the bottle most buyers ask about. Wikipedia says it is flavoured by adding seared French oak staves to the barrel late in ageing, and the producer’s own site calls it French oaked bourbon. In our catalogue the Maker’s Mark 46 is $39.99 at 47% ABV, two points higher than the Original.

The producer’s tasting page for the 46 sits behind an age check, so we have not quoted its notes. In general, the idea of adding oak staves is to bring extra wood flavour, so expect a more oak-forward and spicier version of the Original. If you cannot decide between them, buy both and taste side by side, which is the same test we suggest for any pair of related bottles.

Cask Strength, Cellar Aged and RC6

Three more bottles round out the range:

  • Cask Strength ($64.99, 55.1%). The producer lists a Cask Strength bourbon in its range. At 55.1% it is well above the 45% of Original, so it drinks bolder and takes water well. Our guide to cask strength vs barrel proof explains what the term means.
  • Cellar Aged ($99.99, 45%). The producer lists a Cellar Aged bourbon among its limited releases. Our catalogue lists it as an aged expression. Check the producer’s own page for the details of each release.
  • RC6 Limited Release ($79.99, 45%). Our catalogue lists the maker’s mark rc6 limited release in its wood finish series. Limited releases change from year to year, so check the product page for what is available.

The producer’s site also lists a Maker’s Mark 101 Special Proof, a Wood Finishing Series and a Private Selection, which shows how wide the range has grown.

Which Bottle Should You Buy?

A simple guide:

  • First bottle, mixing or sipping: Maker’s Mark Original at $29.99.
  • More wood and a little more strength: Maker’s 46 at $39.99.
  • You like high proof: Cask Strength at $64.99.
  • A gift or a collection: RC6 at $79.99 or Cellar Aged at $99.99.

Before you order, check the strength and price on the product page. Browse more in our bourbon collection.
